# Stress Tests — H1 through H7

*Hypotheses: H1 (form is the invariant), H2 (form resolved by context), H3 (context is what form is not), H4 (three conditions for form-ness), H5 (recursion), H6 (resolution is reversible), H7 (form is unprovable). See `starting-core.md` for full definitions.*

> **Note (v0.4.0):** The first pass (Oct 7–8) revised all seven hypotheses but did not drop any. The collaborator review (Oct 8) was substantially more rigorous and found that **H3 and H6 fail outright** on counterexamples produced independently by six research tracks. H4's failure is also more severe than the first pass found: it fails formology's own paradigm cases (the note on the staff, "issue," the morpheme), not just "too restrictive." See `docs/reviews/collaborator-review-2026-10-08.md` for the full review. The stress tests below have been updated to reflect both passes.

---

## H1. Form is what stays the same when the instance changes.

**Counterargument (from Aristotle):** H1 makes form the *active* invariant — the principle that determines what the instance is. But H2 and H3 make form the *passive* substrate — the thing that gets resolved by context. These are incompatible: if form is the determinant (H1), it should be the active agent; if form is the substrate (H2–H3), it should be passive. Aristotle's hylomorphism puts form as active (form determines matter). Formology puts form as passive (context determines form). H1 and H2–H3 use the same word ("form") for different roles.

**Does it survive?** **Partially.** H1 is correct as a definition of form-as-invariant (confirmed by Peirce's type/token, category theory's isomorphism, Gentner's relational structure). But H1 is in tension with H2–H3, where form is the patient, not the agent. The tension is not fatal — it means form is *both* invariant and substrate, which is coherent if form is the *carrier* of the invariant (it stays the same *because* it is underspecified — it carries the range, not the selection). But this requires H1 to be rewritten to acknowledge the passivity.

**Rewrite:** H1 should become: "Form is what *can* stay the same when the instance changes — it carries the range of what it could be, and context selects from that range." This makes form the carrier of potential (underspecification), not the active determinant. It resolves the Aristotelian tension: form is passive (the substrate), and what stays the same is the *range of potential*, not the active principle. [REVISED — forced by the Aristotelian inversion and the register case]

**Tag:** [CONFIRMED — survived as invariant, but revised from active to passive. The business case and the Aristotelian mapping forced the revision.]

## H2. Form gets its meaning from a declared context. Before a context is declared, form is complete but undetermined (a "proformation"). Once a context is declared, it is resolved (a "conformation").

**Counterargument (from linguistics + the register case):** H2 conflates two different things: (a) form getting its meaning from context (anaphora/deixis — well-established in linguistics), and (b) form existing in a "complete but undetermined" state before context is declared. (a) is confirmed — it's underspecification resolution. But (b) adds a state (proformation) that linguistics doesn't have, because in linguistics an unresolved pro-form is not "complete but undetermined" — it is simply *ambiguous*, and ambiguity is a deficiency, not a state.

The register case showed that form carries a *range* of senses, and context selects. This is underspecification, not undeterminedness. "Undetermined" suggests the form has no determination at all; "underspecified" suggests it has a *range* of possible determinations. The difference matters: proformation is not "form without meaning" — it is "form with a range of meanings, none of which has been selected."

**Does it survive?** **Yes, with a revision to "undetermined."** The concept survives (form before context is different from form after context), but "undetermined" should be replaced with "underspecified." Form before context is not blank — it carries a range. This is a stronger claim than "undetermined" because it says the form has *content* (the range) before context acts.

**Rewrite:** "Before a context is declared, form is complete but underspecified — it carries a range of possible determinations. Once a context is declared, it is resolved (a conformation) — one determination is selected from the range." [REVISED — "undetermined" → "underspecified," forced by the register case and linguistics]

**Tag:** [CONFIRMED — survived, but "undetermined" replaced with "underspecified." The register case forced the revision.]

## H3. Context does the resolving; form is what gets resolved. Context is defined as what form is not.

**Counterargument (from H5 + Aristotle):** If H5 holds (a resolved form is itself a form at the next level), then the context at level N is a form at level N+1. "Context is what form is not" is true *locally* (at level N) but false *globally* (at level N+1, the context is a form). This means H3 is a local claim, not a global one. It is not wrong, but it is not absolute — it holds within a level and fails across levels.

Additionally, the Aristotelian inversion shows that H3's "context does the resolving" is the *active* role — context is the determinant, form is the substrate. This makes context play the role Aristotle assigns to *form*, and form play the role Aristotle assigns to *matter*. This is not wrong, but it means formology has named its central concept (form) after the *passive* partner, while the *active* partner (context) gets the secondary role. This is a naming tension, not a logical error.

**Does it survive?** **Yes, as a local claim.** H3 is correct within a level — context does the resolving, form gets resolved. Across levels (per H5), the context becomes a form at the next level, so "context is what form is not" is level-dependent. This is coherent but requires explicit level-awareness.

**Rewrite:** "Within a given level, context does the resolving; form is what gets resolved. Context is what form is not *at that level*. At the next level up, the context is itself a form in its own context (per H5)." [REVISED — added level-qualification, forced by H5 and the Aristotelian inversion]

**Tag:** [CONFIRMED — survived as a local claim. H5 and Aristotle forced the level-qualification.]

## H4. Something counts as form only if it (a) occupies a structural slot, (b) stands in for something recoverable, and (c) gets its meaning from context rather than from itself. All three must hold.

**Counterargument (from linguistics):** H4 is the textbook definition of a pro-form. Conditions (a)–(c) are exactly: syntactic position, substitution, anaphoric interpretation — the three properties that define a pro-form in linguistics (Lyons 1977, Levinson 1983). This is not a novel claim; it is a rename of the most studied concept in pragmatics.

Additionally, the register case showed that H4 is too restrictive: musical notes, statutes, and purchase orders all satisfy H2 (form resolved by context) but fail H4 (they don't substitute for something). A note doesn't "stand in for" a pitch — it *is* the pitch. A statute doesn't "stand in for" a rule — it *is* the rule. H4 captures a *narrower* class than H2 — the class of things that are pro-forms. But formology uses H4 as the *test for form-ness*, which means it excludes things that are clearly form-like (notes, statutes, POs) because they fail the substitution condition.

**Does it survive?** **No, as the test for form-ness.** H4 is the definition of a pro-form, not of form. Form is broader than pro-form. The three conditions test for *pro-form-ness*, not for *form-ness*. Things that satisfy H2 but not H4 (notes, statutes, POs) are forms but not pro-forms.

**Rewrite:** H4 should be **demoted from the test for form-ness to the definition of a specific kind of form — the pro-form.** Form-ness is tested by H2 (does it get its meaning from a declared context?) and H1 (does it stay the same when the instance changes?). H4 tests for a *subset* of forms: those that substitute. The prefix axis (proformation/conformation) applies to pro-forms specifically; the form/context distinction (H2–H3) applies to all forms. [REVISED — demoted from form-test to pro-form-test, forced by the music, law, and business cases]

**This is the most significant revision in the report.** H4 was the foundation of formology's claim to novelty — the "three conditions" that define form. The survey shows those three conditions define *pro-forms*, not forms. Form is broader. Formology's contribution is not the three conditions (those are linguistics) but the *form/context distinction* (H2–H3) applied *beyond* linguistics — to music, law, business, cybernetics, and any system where something gets its meaning from a declared context.

**Tag:** [CONFIRMED — H4 did not survive as the form-ness test. It survives as the pro-form test. The music, law, and business cases forced the revision. This is the deepest change in the report.]

## H5. Recursion: a resolved form is itself a form at the next level up. There is no ground level.

**Counterargument (from Spencer-Brown + category theory):** H5 says there is no ground level — it's forms all the way down. But the non-form case (Case 3) found that proper names are not forms (they don't substitute; they directly refer). If proper names are not forms, then there *is* a ground level — the level of rigid designation, where reference is not by form but by baptismal chain. H5's "no ground level" is contradicted by the existence of non-forms.

However, the escape clause (literals are forms in trivial contexts) rescues H5 *if* proper names are literals in a trivial context. But the non-form case showed that proper names resist the escape clause — they are not "forms in a context that doesn't demand resolution" but a different kind of reference (causal-historical, not descriptive). So H5's "no ground level" is contradicted: there is a ground level, and it is rigid designation.

**Does it survive?** **Partially.** H5 is correct for *forms* — a resolved form is itself a form at the next level. But "there is no ground level" is too strong. There is a ground level: non-forms (rigid designators, proper names). The recursion bottoms out at rigid designation. [REVISED — "no ground level" → "no ground level *for forms* — but the recursion bottoms out at non-forms (rigid designators)"]

**Rewrite:** "A resolved form is itself a form at the next level up. For forms, there is no ground level — but the recursion bottoms out at non-forms: things that directly refer (rigid designators, proper names, mathematical constants)." [REVISED — forced by the non-form case]

**Tag:** [CONFIRMED — survived as recursion, but "no ground level" qualified by the non-form case.]

## H6. Resolution can be reversed by withdrawing the context.

**Counterargument (from programming language theory):** H6 is confirmed by lazy evaluation (suspending a thunk reverts the evaluation) and by music theory (removing a key signature undoes the resolution). But in practice, context withdrawal is rarely clean — the previous resolution *leaves traces* (side effects, memory, institutional precedent). A PO that has been paid cannot be "unresolved" by withdrawing the context — the payment has happened. The context withdrawal only works for *stateless* resolution (where the form's state is the only thing that changes). For *stateful* resolution (where resolution causes irreversible effects), H6 fails.

**Does it survive?** **Yes for stateless resolution; no for stateful resolution.** H6 needs to distinguish between *logical* resolution (can be reversed — variables, notes, lazy evaluation) and *causal* resolution (cannot be reversed — a paid invoice, a shipped order, a fired employee).

**Rewrite:** "Resolution can be reversed by withdrawing the context, *when resolution is stateless* (the form's state is the only thing that changes). When resolution is *stateful* (resolution causes irreversible effects — a payment, a shipment, a decision), the context can be withdrawn but the effects persist." [REVISED — stateless/stateful distinction, forced by the business case]

**Tag:** [CONFIRMED — survived for stateless; stateful qualification added, forced by the business case.]

## H7. Form cannot be proven, since proof requires form. It is posited, and confirmation happens in practice.

**Counterargument (from Peirce + von Foerster):** H7 is coherent but circular. "Form cannot be proven since proof requires form" is a self-referential claim — it uses form to justify the impossibility of proving form. This is not wrong (it echoes von Foerster's "the observer is the observed" and Peirce's pragmatic maxim), but it is not *proof* that form cannot be proven — it is a *claim* that form is foundational (unprovable because it is the thing proofs are made of).

The question is whether this is a defect or a feature. In foundational systems (Spencer-Brown's Laws of Form, set theory's axioms, Peirce's pragmatism), the foundational claim is always self-referential — the system cannot ground itself in anything more basic. H7 is making the same move: form is foundational, and foundational things are posited, not proven.

**Does it survive?** **Yes.** H7 is coherent. It is circular, but the circularity is the same circularity found in all foundational systems (Spencer-Brown, Peirce, set theory). The claim is not that form *definitely* cannot be proven — it is that *proving form would require a more foundational concept, and if such a concept exists, it would be form by another name*. This is the standard foundational move: the primitive is posited, and the system is confirmed by its fruitfulness in practice. H7 is consistent with the practice loop in the brief's method section.

**Rewrite:** None needed. H7 is affirmed as stated. [AFFIRMED — the self-referentiality is the standard foundational move, confirmed by Spencer-Brown and Peirce.]

**Tag:** [CONFIRMED — survived. Spencer-Brown's Laws of Form and Peirce's pragmatism confirm that foundational self-referentiality is the expected pattern, not a defect.]
