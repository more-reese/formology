# Field Survey — Tiered by Depth of Shared Form

*Each field examined through formology's practice loop: take it in its own language, render its central ideas as forms and contexts, compare at the level where the two become comparable, bring findings back.*

## Deep Tier

### G. Spencer-Brown — Laws of Form

**Source:** *Laws of Form* (1969). Primary text.

**In its own language:** Spencer-Brown begins with a single primitive — the **mark** — which is simultaneously a distinction (drawing a boundary), an indication (pointing at something), and an operation (calling a state into being). The two equations that govern the mark are:

1. The law of calling: `f f = f` (repeating a name makes no difference)
2. The law of crossing: `f f = ` (crossing a boundary twice returns to the original state)

From these two equations, Spencer-Brown derives Boolean algebra, then the arithmetic of natural numbers, then the calculus of indications. The mark is both content and instruction — it indicates *and* operates. The system is self-referential: the mark distinguishes a space that includes the mark itself.

**Rendered as forms and contexts:** The mark is form in the most literal sense — it is what exists when a distinction is made. But it is also the act of distinguishing. The mark is undetermined before it is drawn (no context declared), and determined once it is drawn (context declared). This is H2's proformation → conformation, but with a critical difference: in Spencer-Brown, the mark *is* the distinction. There is no form separate from the act of distinguishing. Form and context are born together.

**The fork:** Formology's fork from Spencer-Brown is narrow and precise. Both begin with a single primitive that gets its meaning from an act of distinction. Both are recursive (a distinction can be made within a distinction). Both are foundational (meant to underlie other systems, not rest on them). But:

1. **Formology separates form from context.** Spencer-Brown does not — the mark is both the content distinguished and the act of distinguishing. Formology says form is what gets resolved; context is what does the resolving. Spencer-Brown says the mark is both at once. [RIGOROUS]

2. **Formology adds the proformation/conformation distinction.** Spencer-Brown has no "before context" state — the mark either exists or it doesn't. Formology says form can exist before context is declared, in a state of being complete but undetermined. This is formology's addition. [AFFIRMED]

3. **Formology's H4 (the three conditions) has no equivalent in Spencer-Brown.** The mark has no "structural slot" to occupy — it *creates* the space it occupies. The conditions for form-ness in formology are more restrictive than Spencer-Brown's mark, which is unconditional. [AFFIRMED]

4. **Formology's H7 (form cannot be proven) echoes Spencer-Brown directly.** Spencer-Brown's system is self-referential and cannot be grounded in anything more basic — the mark is posited, not derived. Formology's H7 says the same thing about form. [RESONANCE]

**Fork depth:** **Deep.** The overlap holds through the foundational move (a single primitive, meaning by distinction, recursion) and only diverges at the separation of form from context — which is formology's specific contribution. Spencer-Brown is the closest "already exists" candidate, but formology is not a rename of Laws of Form. It is a restriction (adding conditions) and an extension (adding the proformation state and the form/context separation).

**Verdict on overlap:** **Partial overlap with reformulation that adds the proformation/conformation distinction and the three-condition test.** Formology is Spencer-Brown with a constraint (form must satisfy H4) and an added state (proformation — form before context, which Spencer-Brown does not have because the mark creates its own context).

### Linguistics — Pro-forms, Anaphora, Deixis

**Sources:** Lyons, *Semantics* (1977); Bühler's deixis; Halliday & Hasan, *Cohesion in English* (1976); Levinson, *Pragmatics* (1983).

**In its own language:** A **pro-form** is a word that substitutes for another expression. Three properties define it: (a) it occupies a syntactic position (a structural slot), (b) it substitutes for an antecedent (a recoverable expression), (c) its interpretation is anaphoric — it gets its referent from context, not from its own content. **Deixis** is the complementary case: the referent is determined by the speech situation (speaker, time, place) rather than by a prior text. **Anaphora** is resolution by prior text; **cataphora** is resolution by following text.

**Rendered as forms and contexts:** A pro-form is the textbook case of H4. The three conditions (structural slot, substitution, anaphoric interpretation) are *exactly* the linguistic definition. This is the known issue from the reasoner review: "form" and "proformation" may both reduce to "pro-form."

**The fork:** The fork is sharp and uncomfortable:

1. **H4 is the linguistic definition of a pro-form.** Not a reformulation — a direct mapping. [RIGOROUS] Form's three conditions (a, b, c) are pro-form's three conditions (syntactic position, substitution, anaphoric interpretation). This is not a rename of something obscure; it is a rename of the central definition of one of the most studied concepts in linguistics.

2. **Proformation is the pro-form before its antecedent is known.** In linguistics, this is simply an unresolved pro-form — "he arrived" before you know who "he" is. It is not a distinct state in linguistics; it is the same word in a state of temporary ambiguity. Formology elevates this to a named state (proformation), which adds terminology but not content. [AFFIRMED]

3. **Conformation is the pro-form after resolution.** "He arrived. John walked in." — "he" is now resolved to "John." This is anaphora resolution, a well-studied process in computational linguistics (e.g., Hobbs' algorithm, centering theory). [RIGOROUS]

4. **Deixis is the complementary case to anaphora** — the context is the speech situation rather than prior text. Formology's H2 (form gets its meaning from a declared context) covers both anaphora and deixis under one umbrella: "declared context" includes both textual context (anaphora) and situational context (deixis). This is a genuine generalization — linguistics keeps them separate because the resolution mechanisms differ, but formology's abstraction (context resolves form) subsumes both. [AFFIRMED]

5. **H5 (recursion) has a linguistic parallel:** a pronoun can itself be the antecedent of another pronoun ("John said that he saw Mary and that she left. He was angry." — "he" resolves to "John," but the second "he" could resolve to the first "he" in some readings). However, linguistics does not claim this is *the same form at a different level* — it claims it is the same mechanism (anaphora) applied again. [RESONANCE]

**Fork depth:** **Deep at H4, shallow at H2.** The overlap is deepest exactly where the known issue predicted: H4 is linguistics. The proformation/conformation distinction adds terminology (proformation = unresolved pro-form, conformation = resolved pro-form) but the mechanism is anaphora resolution. What formology adds beyond linguistics:

- The generalization from textual context (anaphora) and situational context (deixis) to a single concept of "declared context" — this is formology's contribution, not linguistics'.
- The connection to VSM and cybernetics — formology places the pro-form mechanism in a systems-theory context that linguistics does not.
- The recursion as identity (same form at every level) rather than as mechanism repetition.

**Verdict:** H4 is a **rename of the linguistic pro-form definition.** H2 is a **partial overlap with anaphora/deixis that generalizes from textual+situational context to "declared context" broadly.** The prefix axis (proformation/conformation) is **a rename of anaphora resolution states** with the addition that "before resolution" is a named state rather than a temporary ambiguity.

This is the most important finding of the survey: **the core of formology (H4) is the linguistic definition of a pro-form.** The question is whether the surrounding framework (H1–H3, H5–H7, the VSM connection, the productive morphology) adds enough to justify the new terminology. The brief says "mostly a rename" is acceptable. H4 alone is mostly a rename. The question is what the rest adds.

### Frege — Sense and Reference

**Source:** Frege, "Über Sinn und Bedeutung" (1892).

**In its own language:** Frege distinguished two aspects of meaning: **sense** (Sinn) — the mode of presentation, the "how" of referring — and **reference** (Bedeutung) — the actual object picked out. "The morning star" and "the evening star" have the same reference (Venus) but different senses (different modes of presentation). Sense determines reference; not every expression has a reference, but every meaningful expression has a sense.

**Rendered as forms and contexts:** Sense is what form carries — the structural position, the mode of presentation. Reference is what context supplies — the actual object the form resolves to. In formology terms: form provides the sense; context provides the reference. Proformation is an expression with sense but no reference yet (no context declared). Conformation is an expression with both sense and reference (context declared).

**The fork:**

1. **Sense/reference maps cleanly onto form/context.** [RIGOROUS] Frege's sense is the form's contribution; Frege's reference is the context's contribution. The division of labor is the same: the expression carries one part (sense/form), the world carries the other (reference/context).

2. **But Frege has no proformation state.** An expression with sense but no reference is *undefined* in Frege — "the least even number greater than 2" has a sense but no reference (there is no such number). Formology calls this proformation — a form that exists but hasn't been resolved. Frege calls it a failed reference. [RIGOROUS] This is a genuine difference: formology treats the unresolved state as a real state; Frege treats it as a gap or failure.

3. **H4's three conditions map to sense, not reference.** The structural slot, substitution, and anaphoric interpretation are all properties of *sense* — they describe how the expression means, not what it means. Frege would say these are sense-conditions. [AFFIRMED]

**Fork depth:** **Deep at the sense/reference division, diverging at the treatment of unresolved reference.** The fork is narrow: formology and Frege agree on the division of meaning into expression-carried and context-supplied parts. They diverge on whether the unresolved state is a real state (formology: yes, proformation) or a failure (Frege: no, undefined reference).

**Verdict:** Formology's form/context distinction is a **reformulation of Frege's sense/reference distinction that adds the proformation state** — the idea that sense-without-reference is not a failure but a legitimate state of being "complete but undetermined."

### Peirce — Type and Token, Semiotics

**Sources:** Peirce, *Collected Papers* (1931–1958); specifically the type/token distinction and the semiotic triad.

**In its own language:** Peirce distinguished **type** (the general form — "the word 'the'") from **token** (the concrete instance — "the 7th word in this sentence is 'the'"). Every token instantiates a type; a type is what remains the same across its tokens. Peirce's semiotic triad: **representamen** (the sign), **object** (what it stands for), **interpretant** (the effect or meaning produced). The sign relation is irreducibly triadic — a sign is only a sign if it stands for something *to someone*.

**Rendered as forms and contexts:** Type is form (what stays the same when the instance changes — H1). Token is the instance. The semiotic triad maps: representamen = form (the structural carrier), object = the referent (what context supplies), interpretant = the resolution (the meaning that emerges when form meets context). Proformation is a type with no token yet (or a representamen with no interpretant). Conformation is a type with a token (a sign act in operation).

**The fork:**

1. **Type/token is H1 directly.** [RIGOROUS] "Form is what stays the same when the instance changes" is the type/token distinction stated in different words. This is the second "already exists" match (after pro-form for H4).

2. **The semiotic triad adds a third element formology doesn't have.** [RIGOROUS] Peirce's interpretant — the *effect* of the sign, the meaning as produced in an interpreter — has no direct correspondent in formology. Formology has form (the carrier) and context (the resolver), but no explicit role for the *product* of resolution. The conformation *is* the result, but it is also the form-in-context; Peirce keeps the interpretant separate from both sign and object. Formology collapses what Peirce keeps triadic.

3. **H7 echoes Peirce's pragmatism.** [RESONANCE] Peirce's pragmatic maxim: the meaning of a concept is its practical consequences. Formology's H7: form is confirmed in practice, not proven. The resonance is real but imprecise — Peirce is making a claim about *meaning*, formology about *justification*.

**Fork depth:** **Deep at type/token (H1), middle at the semiotic triad.** The overlap on H1 is exact — a rename. The semiotic triad reveals something formology lacks: a place for the *product* of resolution as distinct from the *state* of being resolved.

**Verdict:** H1 is a **rename of Peirce's type/token distinction.** The semiotic triad reveals a **gap in formology: no explicit role for the interpretant (the product of resolution as distinct from the resolved form).**

### Aristotle — Hylomorphism

**Sources:** Aristotle, *Metaphysics* (Books Z–H); *Physics* (Book II).

**In its own language:** Aristotle distinguished **matter** (hylē) — the potential, the indeterminate substrate — from **form** (eidos) — the actuality, the determining principle. A bronze sphere: bronze is matter, sphereness is form. Form is not a shape imposed on matter but the principle that makes matter *be* something. Matter can receive form; form actualizes matter's potential. The compound (substance) is matter informed by form.

**Rendered as forms and contexts:** Aristotle's *form* is formology's form — the determining principle, what stays the same when the instance changes (H1). Aristotle's *matter* is the potential — the indeterminate substrate that form determines. This maps to proformation: matter is form not yet determined, awaiting form. But there is a critical inversion: in Aristotle, form *acts on* matter to determine it. In formology, context *acts on* form to resolve it. The direction of determination is reversed.

**The fork:**

1. **Form as the determining principle maps to H1.** [RIGOROUS] Both Aristotle and formology say form is what stays the same, what makes the instance be what it is.

2. **The direction of action reverses.** [RIGOROUS] In Aristotle: form acts on matter (form determines matter). In formology: context acts on form (context resolves form). This is not a minor difference — it is an inversion of the causal direction. In hylomorphism, form is the active agent; in formology, form is the passive patient (context does the resolving, H3).

3. **Matter is not context.** [RIGOROUS] Aristotle's matter is the substrate *receiving* form. Formology's context is the *environment* that resolves form. These are not the same role: matter is what form acts *upon*; context is what acts *upon* form. The mapping would be: Aristotle's form = formology's context, and Aristotle's matter = formology's form. This is a clean mapping but it means **formology's "form" is Aristotle's "matter"** — the passive, indeterminate substrate — and **formology's "context" is Aristotle's "form"** — the active, determining principle. This is an inversion that the treatise does not acknowledge.

**Fork depth:** **Deep, with an inversion.** The concepts map but the roles reverse. Formology's form is Aristotle's matter (the indeterminate); formology's context is Aristotle's form (the determinant). The names are swapped. Whether this is a problem or a productive tension depends on whether formology *means* form as the determinant or form as the substrate. H1 says "form is what stays the same when the instance changes" — which is the determinant (the type, the principle). But H2 and H3 say form *gets resolved* by context — which is the substrate (the patient, the matter).

**Verdict:** **H1 follows Aristotle (form = determinant). H2–H3 invert Aristotle (form = substrate, context = determinant).** The tension is internal to formology: H1 makes form active (it determines); H2–H3 make form passive (it gets resolved). This is a real stress test finding — see the stress test on H1 vs. H2–H3.

### Category Theory

**Sources:** Mac Lane, *Categories for the Working Mathematician* (1971); Awodey, *Category Theory* (2006).

**In its own language:** A category consists of objects and morphisms (arrows) between them, with identity arrows and composition. A **functor** maps between categories, preserving structure. A **natural transformation** maps between functors. The key insight: structure is defined by *what is preserved* under mappings, not by the internal constitution of objects. Two objects are "the same" (isomorphic) if there are arrows between them that compose to identity.

**Rendered as forms and contexts:** Form is what is preserved under context change — the invariant across conformations. Context is the mapping (the functor) that transforms form from one conformation to another. Proformation is an object before a functor maps it; conformation is the object after the functor acts.

**The fork:**

1. **H1 is a category-theoretic statement.** [RIGOROUS] "Form is what stays the same when the instance changes" is the definition of an invariant — the thing preserved by a functor across objects. Isomorphism is the formal version: two objects share form if there is an invertible arrow between them.

2. **But category theory has no "proformation" state.** [RIGOROUS] Objects are not "before" or "after" a functor acts — the functor maps between categories, and the object in the source category is not "undetermined" relative to the target. The functor doesn't "resolve" the object; it maps it. Formology's proformation (complete but undetermined) has no category-theoretic correspondent.

3. **Context as functor is a stretch.** [RESONANCE] Context is not a structure-preserving map — it is more like an environment that provides values. The functor metaphor is suggestive but not rigorous: context doesn't preserve form's structure, it *provides* form's reference.

**Fork depth:** **Deep at H1 (invariance), shallow at H2 (no proformation state).** The overlap is real and strong at the level of "form = invariant," but category theory's framework is more general and does not need the proformation/conformation distinction.

**Verdict:** H1 is a **rename of the category-theoretic concept of an invariant/isomorphism class.** The proformation/conformation distinction is **formology's addition, not found in category theory.**

### Second-Order Cybernetics — von Foerster, Beer

**Sources:** von Foerster, *Observing Systems* (1981); Beer, *Brain of the Firm* (1972), *Platform for Change* (1975); Beer, *Diagnosing the System for Organizations* (1985).

**In its own language:** Second-order cybernetics: the observer is part of the system observed. Von Foerster: "the observer is the observed." Beer's VSM: five interacting subsystems (S1–S5), recursive at every level, where S4 (intelligence) observes the environment, S3 (control) manages the internal operations, S5 (policy) holds identity. The environment is not a system — it is what the system is not. The system adapts to the environment through S4's afferent signals and acts on the environment through S1's efferent actions.

**Rendered as forms and contexts:** VSM's mapping to formology is established in the existing work (see starting-core context). The key question for the survey is: does formology add anything beyond VSM?

1. **Formology's form/context is VSM's system/environment.** [RIGOROUS] H3 ("context is what form is not") is Beer's "the environment is what the system is not" (Beer, *Diagnosing the System*, 1985, p. 7). This is a direct mapping.

2. **Formology adds the proformation state to VSM.** [AFFIRMED] VSM has no concept of "the system before the environment has acted on it" — the system is always already in an environment. Formology's proformation is a state VSM does not name: the system as potential, before the environment has demanded adaptation.

3. **Formology's H5 (recursion) is VSM's recursion.** [RIGOROUS] "Each S1 is itself a viable system" is exactly "a resolved form is itself a form at the next level up."

4. **Formology's H7 echoes von Foerster.** [RESONANCE] "Form cannot be proven since proof requires form" echoes "the observer is the observed" — the system cannot stand outside itself to verify its own foundations. But von Foerster is making a claim about observation; H7 is about proof. The resonance is real but imprecise.

**Fork depth:** **Deep.** This is formology's home territory — the framework emerged from VSM. The overlap holds through the system/environment distinction, the recursion, and the foundational posture. Formology adds the proformation state and the productive morphology (suffix/prefix axes) that VSM does not have. But VSM provides the framework that formology is built on.

**Verdict:** Formology is a **reformulation of VSM that adds the proformation state and the productive morphology.** It is not a rename — the additions are real — but the foundation is VSM's system/environment distinction and recursion. The brief says formology "is meant to be more foundational than VSM." The survey suggests it is more *general* (proformation extends to contexts VSM doesn't address) but not more *foundational* (it rests on the same system/environment distinction).

### Gentner — Structure-Mapping Theory

**Source:** Gentner, "Structure-mapping: A Theoretical Framework for Analogy" (1983); Gentner & Markman, "Structure Mapping in Analogical Reasoning" (1997).

**In its own language:** Analogy is the mapping of relational structure from a **base** (source domain) to a **target** (destination domain). The key principle: relations are mapped preferentially over attributes (the relational shift). "The atom is like the solar system" maps the *relational structure* (things orbiting a central body) from solar system to atom, while discarding attributes (size, color, material). Good analogies preserve systematic structure — connected systems of relations.

**Rendered as forms and contexts:** The "form" in formology is precisely what Gentner calls the relational structure — the thing that is preserved across domains when the surface attributes differ. The fork (where two things stop sharing form) is what Gentner calls the limit of the relational mapping — the point where the structure no longer maps.

**The fork:**

1. **H1 is structure-mapping's relational invariant.** [RIGOROUS] "Form is what stays the same when the instance changes" is the relational structure that Gentner's theory identifies as the content of analogy.

2. **The fork is Gentner's mapping limit.** [RIGOROUS] "The point where two things stop sharing form" is the point where the relational structure no longer maps. Formology's "fork" is a rename of a concept Gentner already has.

3. **Gentner has no proformation/conformation distinction.** [AFFIRMED] Structure-mapping is about *mapping between* domains, not about *resolving within* a domain. The proformation/conformation distinction adds a dimension Gentner doesn't address: the state of a structure *before* it has been mapped to a specific context.

4. **Metaphor magnitude (formology's coined term) is Gentner's systematicity.** [RIGOROUS] The claim that metaphor strength is the depth of the fork is Gentner's claim that stronger analogies preserve deeper relational structure. Formology's "metaphor magnitude" is a rename of Gentner's "systematicity" — but with a geometric framing (depth) that Gentner does not use.

**Fork depth:** **Deep at H1 and the fork; no overlap at proformation/conformation.** Gentner provides the most precise prior-art match for the "form = invariant structure" claim and for the "fork = mapping limit" concept. Formology's addition is the resolution-state distinction.

**Verdict:** H1 and the fork are **renames of Gentner's relational invariant and mapping limit.** Metaphor magnitude is a **rename of systematicity.** Formology's addition is the proformation/conformation distinction and the connection to VSM/cybernetics.

### Lakoff and Johnson — Metaphors We Live By

**Source:** Lakoff & Johnson, *Metaphors We Live By* (1980).

**In its own language:** Metaphor is not a figure of speech but a cognitive mechanism: we understand one domain (target) in terms of another (source). "Argument is war" structures how we think about arguments (winning, losing, attacking, defending) by importing the relational structure of war. Conceptual metaphors are systematic mappings between domains. The mapping is partial — it preserves some structure and drops the rest.

**Rendered as forms and contexts:** The shared form between two domains *is* the metaphor. "Argument is war" works because argument and war share a form (adversarial relational structure) at some depth. The fork is where the metaphor breaks ("you can't literally kill an argument's civilians").

**The fork:**

1. **Formology's form is Lakoff's source-domain structure.** [RESONANCE] Not exact — Lakoff emphasizes that the mapping goes one way (source → target), while formology's form is bidirectionally shared. But the core insight — that shared structure underlies metaphor — is the same.

2. **Metaphor magnitude is the depth of the conceptual mapping.** [RESONANCE] Lakoff and Johnson discuss deep metaphors (CONCEPT IS CONTAINER, TIME IS SPACE) that structure vast domains vs. shallow metaphors that are local. The depth of the fork *is* the depth of the metaphor.

3. **Formology adds the resolution-state distinction.** [AFFIRMED] Lakoff and Johnson have no equivalent of proformation — a metaphor that exists but hasn't been bound to a specific target domain.

**Fork depth:** **Middle.** The overlap is real but imprecise. Lakoff and Johnson are making a cognitive claim (metaphor structures thought); formology is making an ontological claim (form is a kind of thing). The shared ground is "structure is what makes analogy/metaphor work," but the frameworks diverge on what structure *is*.

**Verdict:** **Resonance with Lakoff and Johnson's conceptual metaphor theory, but not a direct mapping.** Formology is more abstract (form as a kind of thing) where Lakoff and Johnson are more cognitive (metaphor as a way of thinking). The metaphor magnitude concept resonates with systematicity but is not a rename.

### Structural Realism

**Source:** Worrall, "Structural Realism: The Best of Both Worlds?" (1989); Ladyman, *Every Thing Must Go* (2007).

**In its own language:** Structural realism in philosophy of science: we can know the *structure* of the world but not its *nature*. The continuous part of scientific theory across a theory change (e.g., Newtonian to relativistic mechanics) is the mathematical structure — the relations, not the entities. Poincaré's insight: the equations survive, the entities they describe do not.

**Rendered as forms and contexts:** Form is structure — what survives theory change. Context is the theory — the specific framework that interprets the structure. Proformation is a structure not yet interpreted by a theory; conformation is a structure as interpreted by a specific theory.

**The fork:**

1. **H1 is structural realism's continuity thesis.** [RIGOROUS] "Form is what stays the same when the instance changes" is what structural realism says about scientific theories: structure is what survives.

2. **But structural realism is about knowledge, not being.** [RIGOROUS] Structural realism is an epistemological claim (what we can know) or an ontological claim (what exists). Formology is neither — it is a framework for describing systems. The mapping is at a high level of abstraction: both say "structure is what persists." But structural realism applies this to scientific theory change; formology applies it to any system.

3. **Formology is more general but less precise.** [AFFIRMED] Structural realism has decades of formal development (Ramsey sentences, structural equations, group-theoretic formulations). Formology's H1 is less rigorous than structural realism's formal apparatus.

**Fork depth:** **Middle.** The overlap is at the level of "structure persists" — deep in principle, but the formalisms diverge quickly. Structural realism is a philosophical position about science; formology is a descriptive framework for systems.

**Verdict:** H1 has **partial overlap with structural realism's continuity thesis, but formology is less formally developed and applied to a different domain.**

## Middle Tier

### Programming Language Theory — Variables, Binding, Scope, Lazy Evaluation

**In its own language:** A variable is a name bound to a value. Before binding, the name exists but has no value (an unbound variable). After binding, the name refers to the value. **Scope** is the region of the program where a binding is visible. **Lazy evaluation** (call-by-need) delays the evaluation of an expression until its value is needed — the expression exists as a thunk (a suspended computation) before it is forced.

**Rendered as forms and contexts:** A variable is a form — it has a name (structural slot), substitutes for a value, and gets its meaning from the binding context. An unbound variable is a proformation. A bound variable is a conformation. Lazy evaluation is formology's H6 — a conformation can be reversed to a proformation by withdrawing the context (garbage-collecting the binding and re-suspending the thunk).

**The fork:**

1. **Variables are the cleanest example of H4 outside linguistics.** [RIGOROUS] A variable occupies a structural slot (the expression where it appears), substitutes for a value, and gets its meaning from the binding context. This is the pro-form definition applied to code.

2. **Scope is the declarable context.** [RIGOROUS] A binding is visible within its scope — the declared region where the context applies. Outside the scope, the variable reverts to unbound (H6: resolution can be reversed by withdrawing context). This is a precise match.

3. **Lazy evaluation is H6.** [RIGOROUS] Suspending and forcing a thunk is exactly "withdrawing the context reverses the resolution" and "re-declaring the context re-resolves." This is the strongest confirmation of H6 in any field.

**Fork depth:** **Deep at H4 and H6, shallow at the prefix axis.** Programming language theory confirms H4 (variables are pro-forms) and H6 (lazy evaluation is context withdrawal/re-declaration). But PL theory has no proformation/conformation *states* — the variable is either bound or unbound, and the states are not given names that carry grammatical content.

**Verdict:** PL theory **confirms H4 and H6** through the variable binding/lazy evaluation parallel. The proformation/conformation terminology is a rename of "unbound/bound," but the connection to lazy evaluation as H6 is a genuine insight.

### Music Theory

**In its own language:** A **note** has a pitch class (e.g., "B") and an octave. A note on a staff has no determinate pitch until a **key signature** is declared — in C major, B is a leading tone; in B♭ major, B is the tonic. The same note on the same staff has different musical function depending on the key.

**Rendered as forms and contexts:** The note on the staff is form (it has a position, it stands for a pitch). The key signature is context (it determines the note's function). A note without a key signature is a proformation; a note in a key is a conformation. This is the example given in H2.

**The fork:**

1. **H2's example is accurate.** [RIGOROUS] The note/key-signature example is a real instance of form resolved by context. It confirms H2.

2. **But notes are not pro-forms.** [AFFIRMED] A note does not "stand in for" something — it *is* the pitch (or pitch class). It fails H4's condition (b): substitution. The note is not substituting for a recoverable antecedent; it is the thing itself. This means the music theory example confirms H2 but not H4 — which suggests H2 and H4 are independent, and H4 is more restrictive than H2.

3. **H6 is confirmed.** [RIGOROUS] Removing a key signature from a passage of music undoes the resolution — the notes revert to pitch classes without determinate function. This is context withdrawal.

**Fork depth:** **Middle.** The note/key-signature example is formology's clearest pedagogical illustration of H2 and H6, but it fails H4 (notes don't substitute). This is a useful finding: H2 and H4 are not the same claim, and examples that satisfy H2 may not satisfy H4.

### Law — Statute vs. Application

**In its own language:** A **statute** is a general rule ("no vehicle in the park"). **Application** is the statute resolved by a specific case (is a bicycle a vehicle? is an ambulance?). The statute is undetermined until a case forces its interpretation. The same statute resolves differently in different cases.

**The fork:**

1. **Statute/application is proformation/conformation.** [RIGOROUS] A statute is a form (general, undetermined) that gets resolved by a case (context). This is H2.

2. **But statutes don't satisfy H4.** [AFFIRMED] A statute occupies a structural slot (the law), but it does not "stand in for" something — it *is* the rule. It fails H4's substitution condition.

3. **Legal interpretation has its own resolution mechanism** (hermeneutics, Dworkin's chain novel) that is not form/context resolution but interpretive construction. [RESONANCE]

**Fork depth:** **Middle.** Confirms H2 but not H4. The statute is form-like (general, context-dependent for specific meaning) but not a pro-form (no substitution). Same finding as music theory: H2 is more general than H4.

### Christopher Alexander — Pattern Language

**Source:** Alexander, *A Pattern Language* (1977); *The Timeless Way of Building* (1979).

**In its own language:** A pattern is a three-part rule: a **context**, a **problem**, and a **solution**. Patterns are relational — each pattern connects to other patterns above it (larger) and below it (smaller). A pattern language is a network of patterns where each pattern's context includes other patterns. The pattern is invariant across specific buildings but resolved by specific sites.

**The fork:**

1. **Alexander's pattern is formology's form.** [RESONANCE] The pattern is the invariant (what stays the same across buildings); the site is the context (what resolves the pattern into a specific design). The fit is suggestive but imprecise — Alexander's patterns are prescriptive (do this), while formology's forms are descriptive (this is what form is).

2. **Alexander's context is formology's context.** [RIGOROUS] Both use "context" to mean the specific situation that resolves the general pattern/form into a specific instance. The word is the same and the meaning is the same.

3. **The pattern language is recursive — each pattern contains patterns.** [RESONANCE] This maps to H5, but Alexander's recursion is compositional (patterns contain patterns) while formology's is resolutive (a resolved form is itself a form at the next level).

**Fork depth:** **Middle.** Alexander's pattern/context is a real resonance with H2, and the recursion resonates with H5. But the frameworks diverge on what patterns *do* (prescribe vs. describe).

### Genotype and Phenotype

**In its own language:** The **genotype** is the genetic code; the **phenotype** is the expressed traits. The same genotype can produce different phenotypes in different environments (phenotypic plasticity). The genotype is the invariant; the environment determines which traits are expressed.

**The fork:**

1. **Genotype/phenotype is form/context.** [RIGOROUS] Genotype = form (the invariant, what stays the same), environment = context (what resolves the genotype into a specific phenotype), phenotype = conformation (the resolved form).

2. **Phenotypic plasticity is H6.** [RIGOROUS] Changing the environment can change the phenotype without changing the genotype — resolution reversed by withdrawing/changing context. Confirmed.

**Fork depth:** **Middle.** The mapping is clean but the concepts are biological — formology generalizes from genetics to any system, which is the value and the risk.

### Saussure

**In its own language:** **Langue** (the language system — the social code) vs. **parole** (the individual speech act). **Signifiant** (signifier — the sound-image) vs. **signifié** (signified — the concept). The sign is the union of signifier and signified. Meaning is differential — a sign means what it does because it is not the other signs.

**The fork:**

1. **Langue/parole is type/token.** [RIGOROUS] Langue is the system (the form); parole is the instance (the token). This is Peirce's type/token, not a new mapping.

2. **Signifier/signified is not form/context.** [AFFIRMED] The signifier is the form of the sign (its sound-image); the signified is its concept (its meaning). But both are *internal* to the sign — they are not the expression/context distinction. The signifier is not "resolved" by the signified; they coexist as two sides of the same coin.

**Fork depth:** **Shallow.** Saussure's sign is not a form resolved by context; it is a bipartite entity whose two parts are inseparable. The langue/parole distinction reduces to type/token, already covered.

## Shallow Tier

### Gestalt Psychology

Gestalt ("whole form") is about perception — the whole is other than the sum of its parts. The gestalt is the perceived form, which is real but not reducible to the elements. [RESONANCE] The gestalt is a form in formology's sense (it stays the same when the elements change — you can change the dots and keep the picture). But Gestalt psychology is about *perception* of wholes, not about *resolution by context*. The fork is shallow because Gestalt has no context mechanism — the form is not resolved by a declared context; it is perceived directly.

### Phenomenology

Husserl's epoché (bracketing) suspends the natural attitude, making the phenomenon appear as phenomenon. [RESONANCE] This is suggestive of H6 (withdrawing context reverses resolution) — the epoché withdraws the assumption of reality, and the phenomenon reverts from "a thing in the world" to "a thing as experienced." But the mapping is imprecise: the epoché is a methodological act, not a context withdrawal. Fork is shallow.

### D'Arcy Thompson — Morphology

Thompson's *On Growth and Form* (1917): form is determined by physical forces. The same mathematical transformation can deform one species' shape into another's. [RIGOROUS] Thompson's form is the invariant under transformation — H1 applies. But Thompson has no context mechanism (form is determined by physics, not resolved by a declared context). Fork is shallow.

### Buddhist Form and Emptiness — Rupa, Sunyata

Rupa (form) and sunyata (emptiness): form is empty of independent existence; form *is* emptiness. The Heart Sutra: "form is exactly emptiness, emptiness exactly form." [RESONANCE] This is a deep resonance with H7 (form cannot be proven, only posited) and H5 (no ground level — emptiness all the way down). But the Buddhist concept is soteriological (a path to liberation), not descriptive (a framework for systems). The frameworks are doing different work. Fork is shallow but the resonance is real — "form is what stays the same when the instance changes" and "form is empty of independent existence" are saying compatible things from very different vantage points.
