# Case Study: REGISTER — "case" across four registers under v0.4.0 rules

*Second practice-level case. Run date: October 8, 2026. Same structure as the "issue" case: take each register in its own language, render as forms and contexts, compare, bring back, hunt for disconfirmations.*

---

## The case

The word "case" as used in four registers:

1. **Lawyer register:** "the case of Smith v. Jones" — a legal proceeding, a dispute brought to court
2. **Doctor register:** "a case of pneumonia" — an instance of a disease, a clinical presentation
3. **Business/register consultant:** "the business case for X" — an argument for doing something, a justification
4. **Everyday register:** "in that case, I'll go" — a situation, a set of circumstances; "that's the case" — that is true

## Step 1: Take each register in its own language

### Lawyer

In the lawyer's language: a "case" is a dispute brought before a court for adjudication. It has parties (plaintiff, defendant), a claim, a set of facts, a procedural posture, and a disposition (decided, settled, dismissed). It is the fundamental unit of adjudication. Cases make law through precedent — a decided case becomes a rule for future cases.

### Doctor

In the doctor's language: a "case" is an instance of a disease or condition in a specific patient. "A case of pneumonia" means: this patient, presenting these symptoms, with this diagnosis. It is the fundamental unit of clinical reporting. Cases make medicine through case series and case reports — individual cases accumulate into evidence.

### Business/consultant

In the business register: a "case" is an argument or justification for a decision. "The business case for investing in X" means: here is why we should do X, with costs, benefits, risks, and alternatives. It is the fundamental unit of organizational decision-making. It is not a proceeding (lawyer) or an instance (doctor); it is an *argument*.

### Everyday

In everyday language: "case" has two main uses: "in that case" (a situation, a set of circumstances — "if it's raining, in that case I'll stay home") and "that's the case" (that is true, that is the state of affairs — "is it the case that you're coming?"). Both are about *situation*, not about proceedings, instances, or arguments.

## Step 2: Render each as forms and contexts

Under the v0.4.0 rules:

### The shared form

What stays the same across all four registers? What is the invariant?

All four uses share: **an instance of a situation considered as a unit.** The lawyer's case is an instance of a dispute considered as a unit. The doctor's case is an instance of a disease considered as a unit. The business case is an instance of a decision-context considered as a unit. The everyday case is an instance of a situation considered as a unit.

Is this enough to be a form? Under H1 v0.4.0: "Form is what stays the same across a declared range of variation, in a declared respect." The range is "the four registers." The respect is "what kind of unit." What stays the same is: **a unit of situation.**

Under H4 v0.4.0: is "case" a placeholder or a pattern?

- **Placeholder** (stands in for a recoverable antecedent)? The lawyer's "case" stands in for "the dispute of Smith v. Jones" — recoverable from the case name. The doctor's "case" stands in for "the patient's presentation" — recoverable from the clinical record. The business "case" stands in for "the argument" — recoverable from the document. The everyday "case" stands in for "the situation" — recoverable from the conversation. In all four, the antecedent is recoverable. "Case" is a placeholder.
- **Pattern** (≥1 parameter open)? Also yes — "what kind of unit" is the open parameter. The word doesn't specify whether it's a legal proceeding, a clinical instance, a business argument, or a situation.

**"Case" satisfies both H4 kinds.** It is both a placeholder (stands in for a recoverable situation) and a pattern (what kind of unit is open). This is stronger than "issue," which was a pattern only (the everyday register broke the placeholder condition — donkey anaphora). "Case" has a recoverable antecedent in all four registers.

This is a difference from the "issue" case. Let me watch whether it matters.

### Does the shared sense do work?

The shared sense is "a unit of situation." Does it do work in each register?

- **Lawyer:** Yes. "A unit of situation" → the lawyer's glossary (procedure, adjudication, precedent) resolves it into "a dispute for adjudication." The shared sense tells you the case is a bounded unit that will be disposed of, not just an ongoing situation.
- **Doctor:** Yes. "A unit of situation" → the doctor's glossary (diagnosis, presentation, patient) resolves it into "an instance of a disease." The shared sense tells you the case is a countable instance, not a general condition.
- **Business:** Yes. "A unit of situation" → the business glossary (investment, ROI, risk) resolves it into "a justification for a decision." The shared sense tells you the case is a bounded argument, not a general discussion.
- **Everyday:** Yes. "A unit of situation" → the everyday glossary (practical reasoning, conversation) resolves it into "the circumstances I'm in." The shared sense tells you "case" is about the specific situation, not a general truth. ("In that case" = "given the situation I just described.")

**The shared sense does work in all four registers.** This is different from the "issue" case, where the everyday register was a boundary case. "Case" holds across all four.

Is this a problem? The "issue" case had a boundary (the everyday register was a proformation/anchor). "Case" doesn't. Does the framework *require* a boundary? No — the framework says the boundary *may* exist, not that it *must*. The absence of a boundary is not a failure; it's a difference.

But: is the everyday "case" really the same form as the lawyer's "case"? "In that case, I'll go" is about a situation; "the case of Smith v. Jones" is about a legal proceeding. Are these the same "unit of situation"?

The lawyer would say: a case is a specific kind of situation — one that has been brought to a court. The everyday speaker would say: "case" just means "situation." The shared sense ("a unit of situation") is thin but present. The question is whether it does enough work.

In the everyday register, "case" is doing real work: it is bounding a situation ("in *that* case" = "given *that specific* situation, not a general one"). The bounding is the work. Without "case," the sentence would be "if it's raining, I'll stay home" — which works, but "in that case" adds a specific rhetorical move: it treats the situation as a discrete unit, not a continuous condition. That is the form doing work.

**The shared sense does work in all four registers. No boundary case.**

### The reader's role (H9)

Register lives in the reader's role and the glossary it brings. What glossary does each reader bring?

- **Lawyer:** the legal glossary (procedure, precedent, parties, claims, disposition)
- **Doctor:** the clinical glossary (diagnosis, presentation, patient, treatment)
- **Business:** the organizational glossary (investment, ROI, risk, alternatives, decision)
- **Everyday:** the practical glossary (situation, circumstances, conversation)

The reader's role determines the register, which determines the reading. This is the same structure as the "issue" case, but all four readers have glossaries. There is no "no glossary" case — the everyday speaker does have a glossary for "case" (the practical reasoning glossary of situation and circumstances).

This is a difference from "issue": the everyday speaker had no glossary for "issue" (it was used idiomatically, "I have issues with that"). The everyday speaker *does* have a glossary for "case" ("in that case" = "in that situation" — the speaker knows what situation they're referring to).

## Step 3: Compare at the level where the cases become comparable

The four registers are comparable at the level of "what kind of unit is 'case'?" This is the open parameter.

| Register | What kind of unit | Resolution system | Form or anchor? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lawyer | A dispute for adjudication | Law/procedure/courts | Pattern + placeholder (conformation) |
| Doctor | An instance of a disease | Clinical/diagnosis/patient | Pattern + placeholder (conformation) |
| Business | An argument for a decision | Organization/ROI/risk | Pattern + placeholder (conformation) |
| Everyday | A situation considered as a unit | Practical reasoning/conversation | Pattern + placeholder (conformation) |

All four are conformations. No boundary case. The form holds across all four registers, and the shared sense does work in all four.

## Step 4: Bring the finding back into each register's own terms

### Lawyer

"'Case' means a dispute brought to court. But the word is broader — a doctor has 'cases,' a business has a 'business case,' and in everyday speech 'in that case' means 'in that situation.' What's common is that 'case' is a unit of something — a unit of dispute for me, a unit of disease for the doctor, a unit of argument for the business, a unit of situation for everyone. The court doesn't decide what kind of unit it is; the reader does."

### Doctor

"A 'case' is a patient with a condition — 'a case of pneumonia.' But the word is used everywhere. A lawyer has cases, a business has a business case, and 'in that case' just means 'in that situation.' What's common is the bounding: a case is a specific instance, bounded, countable. I count cases. The lawyer counts cases. The business counts cases. The everyday speaker bounds situations. The bounding is the form; the disease is the content my register supplies."

### Business

"A 'business case' is a justification for a decision — costs, benefits, risks, alternatives. But the word 'case' is broader. A lawyer has cases, a doctor has cases, 'in that case' means 'in that situation.' What's common is that a 'case' is always a bounded argument: 'here is a specific unit, and here is what should happen with it.' The lawyer argues the dispute. The doctor presents the patient. I present the investment. The everyday speaker says 'given this situation, here's what I'll do.' The form is the bounding; the business context supplies 'investment' as the content."

### Everyday

"'Case' just means a situation — 'in that case, I'll go.' But it's used in law, medicine, business. I don't think about it, but 'case' always seems to mean 'a specific situation, not a general one.' When I say 'in that case,' I'm saying 'given that specific situation' — I'm bounding it. That's what the word does. I don't have a special vocabulary for it — it just means 'situation, but specific.'"

## Step 5: Hunt for disconfirmations

### 1. Register rejection: Do competent users reject the rendering?

**Lawyer:** Would a lawyer reject the claim that "case" is "a unit of situation" resolved by their register into "a dispute for adjudication"? They would likely accept that the word has broader uses and that the legal meaning is one specific reading. The rendering is acceptable.

**Doctor:** Would a doctor reject it? They would accept that "case" means a specific instance and that the clinical meaning is one reading. Acceptable.

**Business:** Would a business person reject it? They would accept that "business case" is a specific use of a broader word. Acceptable.

**Everyday:** Would an everyday speaker reject the claim that "case" means "a situation considered as a unit"? This is what they already say: "in that case" = "in that situation." The rendering is acceptable.

**No register rejection found.**

### 2. Nesting failure: Is a declared nesting shown not to nest?

The nesting: "case" at the register level is a form at the level of "words that bound situations" — the class of situation-bounding terms (also: "scenario," "instance," "example," "case" itself). Is this nesting real?

"Case" is indeed one instance of the higher-level form "words that bound situations." The nesting holds. But: is the higher-level form actually a form, or is it a rename of "noun"? "Scenario" and "instance" and "example" all bound situations, but so does "thing" and "event" and "situation" itself. Is "words that bound situations" just "nouns"?

No — "words that bound situations" is more specific than "nouns." It is the class of words that treat a situation as a discrete, countable, bounded unit — not just any noun. "Situation" (the word) doesn't bound; it names. "Case" bounds; it says "this situation, here, as a unit." The nesting holds at the level of "bounding words," not at the level of "nouns."

**No nesting failure found.**

### 3. Invariance failure: Does an agreed instance lack the claimed invariant?

The claimed invariant: "a unit of situation" (something bounded, countable, specific).

- Lawyer: ✅ A case is a bounded dispute — countable, specific, disposed of as a unit.
- Doctor: ✅ A case is a bounded instance of disease — countable, specific, treated as a unit.
- Business: ✅ A case is a bounded argument — countable, specific, decided as a unit.
- Everyday: ✅ "In that case" bounds a situation — specific, not general, treated as a unit.

The invariant holds in all four registers. No invariance failure.

But: is the invariant too thin? "A unit of situation" could describe almost any noun that refers to a state of affairs. Is the form vacuous at this level?

The form is not vacuous because it is doing work: it is distinguishing "case" (bounded, countable, specific) from "situation" (unbounded, continuous, general). The word "case" adds bounding that "situation" alone doesn't have. The invariant is thin but not empty — it is doing the work of bounding.

**No invariance failure found.** The invariant holds in all four registers and does work (bounding).

## Verdict

**The case survives. No disconfirmation found. Second [CONFIRMED] tag.**

Key differences from the "issue" case:

1. **All four registers are conformations.** Unlike "issue" (where the everyday register was a proformation/anchor), "case" holds across all four. The shared sense ("a unit of situation") does work in every register, including the everyday one. The form is stronger here — it has no boundary case.

2. **"Case" satisfies both H4 kinds.** It is both a placeholder (stands in for a recoverable situation) and a pattern (what kind of unit is open). "Issue" was a pattern only (the everyday register broke the placeholder condition). "Case" is a stronger form — it has the placeholder property that "issue" lacks.

3. **The invariant is thinner but not empty.** "A unit of situation" is thinner than "a thing that demands attention" (the "issue" invariant), but it does real work: it bounds. "Case" adds bounding that "situation" alone doesn't have. The invariant is doing the work of distinguishing "case" from "situation."

4. **The everyday register has a glossary here.** In the "issue" case, the everyday speaker had no glossary ("I have issues with that" is idiomatic). In the "case" register, the everyday speaker does have a glossary ("in that case" = "in that situation" — the speaker knows what situation they're referring to). The presence of an everyday glossary makes "case" a stronger form than "issue."

## What the case reveals

1. **Not all register cases have a boundary.** The "issue" case had a boundary (everyday = proformation/anchor). The "case" case doesn't. The framework correctly handles both: it doesn't *require* a boundary, it *allows* one. The presence or absence of a boundary is a finding, not a prediction.

2. **The two H4 kinds (placeholder + pattern) are independent.** "Case" is both; "issue" is one (pattern only). The placeholder property is not required for form-hood — patterns are forms too. But placeholders are a stronger kind of form, and the case study shows why: they have a recoverable antecedent, which means the resolution is grounded in something outside the word itself.

3. **The invariant's work is bounding, not attention.** In the "issue" case, the invariant was "demands attention." In the "case" case, it is "bounds a situation." These are different invariants for different words. The framework does not require a single invariant across all register cases — it requires that *whatever* invariant is claimed holds in all the registers tested. The invariant is word-specific, not framework-specific.

4. **The everyday register is not always the boundary.** The "issue" case suggested that the everyday register is where forms break down (idiom, no glossary). The "case" case shows this is not always so: the everyday "case" has a glossary and is a conformation. The boundary, when it exists, is about the *absence of a resolution system*, not about informality per se.
