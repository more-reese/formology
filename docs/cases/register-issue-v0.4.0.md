# Case Study: REGISTER — "issue" across four registers under v0.4.0 rules

*Run date: October 8, 2026. This is the first case run under the rewritten core. The goal is not to confirm formology but to try to break it. H7' names three disconfirmations: register rejection (competent users reject the rendering), nesting failure (a declared nesting is shown not to nest), and invariance failure (an agreed instance lacks the claimed invariant). If one appears, the framework fails in practice, not just in theory.*

---

## The case

The word "issue" as used in four registers:

1. **CEO register:** "we have a quality issue" — a problem, a malfunction, something broken that needs fixing
2. **Developer register:** "I created an issue for that" — a neutral unit of tracked work, a ticket in a system
3. **Lawyer register:** "the issue in this case is..." — a point in dispute, a legal question requiring adjudication
4. **Everyday register:** "I have issues with that" — a personal objection, an emotional difficulty, a vague sense of disagreement

## Step 1: Take each register in its own language

### CEO register

In the CEO's language: an "issue" is something that demands attention because something is wrong. It carries negative valence. It is not neutral — it is a call to action. The quality issue is the gap between how things should be and how they are. It demands a fix. The CEO does not "create" issues; they discover them. Issues arrive from the environment (customer complaints, defect reports, audit findings).

### Developer register

In the developer's language: an "issue" is a unit of tracked work in a system (GitHub, Jira, Linear). It has a number, a title, a status (open, in progress, closed), an assignee, labels, and comments. It is neutral — it can be a bug, a feature request, a question, or a task. The developer creates it deliberately. It is not a problem; it is a unit of process. Closing it is not fixing it (though it can be); it is removing it from the active set.

### Lawyer register

In the lawyer's language: an "issue" is a point of law or fact in dispute that the court must decide. It is formalized: "the issue is whether the contract was breached." It is not a problem or a unit of work; it is a *question*. It is settled by adjudication, not by fixing. A case can have multiple issues, each disposed of separately. The issue is the smallest unit of adjudication.

### Everyday register

In everyday language: "I have issues with that" is a vague expression of disapproval or discomfort. It is not a unit of anything — it is a feeling. It does not demand a fix, a ticket, or a legal decision. It demands a conversation, or nothing. It is the least formal register, and the most emotionally loaded.

## Step 2: Render each as forms and contexts

Under the v0.4.0 rules:

- **Form (H1):** What stays the same across the four registers? What is the invariant — the thing that persists when the instance changes?
- **Context (H2):** What is in force for each register? What supplies the reading?
- **Two roles (H3):** In each resolution, form is what is read; context is what it is read against. Both are filled by forms.
- **Two kinds (H4):** Is "issue" a placeholder (stands in for a recoverable antecedent) or a pattern (has at least one parameter left open)? Or is it an anchor (content from itself) or a limit (nothing to read)?
- **Level (H5):** Are there nested resolutions? Is "issue" resolved at one level and then re-resolved at a higher level?
- **Supersession (H6):** Can the resolution be superseded? What trace does the old context leave?
- **Reader (H9):** Register lives in the reader's role and the glossary it brings. What glossary does each reader bring?

### The shared form

The question H1 asks: what stays the same across the four registers?

All four uses share *something*. The CEO's issue, the developer's issue, the lawyer's issue, and the everyday issue are not four random homonyms (like "bank" = river bank / financial bank). They share a thin semantic thread: **a thing that demands attention.**

But is this thread enough to be a form? Under H1 v0.4.0: "Form is what stays the same across a declared range of variation, in a declared respect. The range is set by the comparison level, not by the form." The range here is "the four registers in which 'issue' is used." The respect is "what kind of attention it demands." What stays the same is: **something that occupies a slot in an attention-structure and gets its specific meaning from the register that reads it.**

Under H4 v0.4.0: is "issue" a placeholder or a pattern?

- Does it stand in for a recoverable antecedent (placeholder)? The CEO's "issue" stands in for "the quality problem" — recoverable from context. The developer's "issue" stands in for "the tracked work unit" — recoverable from the ticket system. The lawyer's "issue" stands in for "the point of law in dispute" — recoverable from the case. The everyday "issue" stands in for... what? A vague feeling of disapproval? The antecedent is not clearly recoverable. This is **donkey anaphora** — even real pronouns sometimes lack a recoverable antecedent (the collaborator review flagged this).
- Does it have at least one parameter left open (pattern)? Yes — "what kind of attention" is the open parameter. The word "issue" doesn't specify whether it's a problem to fix, a ticket to track, a question to adjudicate, or a feeling to discuss. That parameter is left open, to be filled by the register.

**Verdict: "issue" is a pattern, not a placeholder.** It has at least one parameter left open (what kind of attention). It does not reliably stand in for a recoverable antecedent (the everyday register breaks this). Under the v0.4.0 split, patterns are forms. So "issue" is a form.

But: is it *one* form or *four*? Under H9: "Whether one form underlies several registers is decided per case by whether the shared sense does work in each register."

### The test: does the shared sense do work?

The shared sense is "a thing that demands attention." Does this do work in each register?

- **CEO:** Yes. "A thing that demands attention" → the CEO's glossary (operations, quality, accountability) resolves it into "a problem to fix." The shared sense does work — it tells you the issue requires action, not just observation.
- **Developer:** Yes, but differently. "A thing that demands attention" → the developer's glossary (process, tracking, workflow) resolves it into "a unit of tracked work." The shared sense does work — it tells you the issue is trackable, not just a feeling.
- **Lawyer:** Yes. "A thing that demands attention" → the lawyer's glossary (adjudication, dispute, procedure) resolves it into "a point to decide." The shared sense does work — it tells you the issue requires a ruling, not a fix.
- **Everyday:** **Maybe not.** "I have issues with that" → does the shared sense ("a thing that demands attention") do work here? The everyday register doesn't demand action, tracking, or adjudication. It demands... what? A conversation? Nothing? The shared sense is doing very little work. The everyday "issue" is closer to "a feeling of disapproval" — which is closer to an anchor (content from itself) than a pattern (parameter left open). The word is being used almost idiomatically, not structurally.

**This is a potential invariance failure (H7' disconfirmation #3).** The shared invariant ("a thing that demands attention") may not hold in the everyday register. If "issues" in "I have issues with that" doesn't demand attention — it's just a vague expression of disapproval — then the invariant is missing, and the everyday register is not a conformation of the same form.

But is this fatal? The v0.4.0 rules say form-hood is "relative to the resolution system in play." Under the everyday register's resolution system, "issue" may be an anchor (idiomatic, content from itself) rather than a pattern. The form-hood is register-dependent. The case doesn't fail — it shows that form-hood is not absolute, which is what H4' already says.

**However:** the first pass said "the everyday 'issue' is a form because it carries a range of senses." The v0.4.0 rules say "a form is fixed but underspecified." Is the everyday "issue" underspecified? Or is it fully specified as "vague disapproval" — in which case it is not underspecified, it is an anchor, and the shared form does not hold across all four registers.

### The resolution

Under the v0.4.0 rules, the honest answer is:

**"Issue" is one form across three registers (CEO, developer, lawyer) and an anchor in the fourth (everyday).** The shared sense ("a thing that demands attention") does work in three of four registers. In the everyday register, it is closer to an idiomatic expression — content from itself, not resolved by context. The form holds across the formal registers and fails in the informal one.

This is not a disconfirmation. H7' says disconfirmation happens when "an agreed instance lacks the claimed invariant." The everyday "issue" is not an agreed instance of the form — it is the case where the form-hood test returns "anchor, not pattern." The framework correctly identifies it as outside the form's range.

But this raises a question: **is the everyday register a fifth register, or is it the absence of a register?** Under H9, register lives in the reader's role and glossary. The everyday speaker has no glossary — they are not a CEO, a developer, or a lawyer. They are using the word without a professional glossary. Is "no glossary" a register? Or is it the absence of one — the context not in force?

Under H2 v0.4.0: "With no context in force, no reading is licensed (proformation)." The everyday "issue" may be a proformation — a form with no context in force to resolve it. It is not resolved; it is just *used*, idiomatically, without a resolution system.

**This is a finding, not a failure.** The framework correctly distinguishes:
- Three conformations (CEO, developer, lawyer — each resolved by a professional glossary)
- One proformation (everyday — no glossary in force, no reading licensed, used idiomatically)

## Step 3: Compare at the level where the cases become comparable

The four registers are comparable at the level of "what kind of attention does 'issue' demand?" This is the open parameter (H4' pattern). At this level:

| Register | What kind of attention | Resolution system | Form or anchor? |
|---|---|---|---|
| CEO | Fix this (action) | Operations/quality/accountability | Pattern (conformation) |
| Developer | Track this (process) | GitHub/Jira/workflow | Pattern (conformation) |
| Lawyer | Decide this (adjudication) | Law/procedure/courts | Pattern (conformation) |
| Everyday | ??? (vague disapproval) | None (no professional glossary) | Proformation or anchor |

The three formal registers are conformations of one form, resolved by three different contexts (three professional glossaries). The everyday register is either a proformation (no context in force) or an anchor (content from itself — the idiomatic expression "have issues with" is a fixed phrase).

## Step 4: Bring the finding back into each register's own terms

### In the CEO's terms

"The word 'issue' means different things depending on who's talking. When I say it, I mean something's broken. When my CTO says it, they mean a ticket. When my lawyer says it, they mean a question for a judge. When my friend says it, they're just venting. The word is the same; the reader's role determines the meaning."

### In the developer's terms

"'Issue' is a polymorphic type. The type signature is `issue<AttentionType>` where `AttentionType` is inferred from the reader's context. The CEO's `AttentionType` is `ActionRequired`. Mine is `TrackableUnit`. The lawyer's is `QuestionForAdjudication`. The everyday speaker's is... `Void` — no type parameter supplied, so it falls back to the idiomatic default. The type system correctly refuses to resolve it to a formal type."

### In the lawyer's terms

"The word 'issue' has different meanings in different contexts — this is the standard problem of interpretation. The legal meaning is a point in dispute. The ordinary meaning varies. What matters is who is speaking and in what capacity — the reader's role determines the sense. An 'issue' spoken by a CEO in a board meeting is not the same 'issue' spoken by a friend over dinner. The form is the same; the register supplies the construction."

### In the everyday register's terms

"'Issues' is just... a word people use. I don't know what it 'really' means — it means different things in different situations. When my boss says 'quality issue' I know what they mean. When I say 'I have issues with that' I just mean I don't like it. It's not that deep."

(And this is where the everyday register confirms the framework: the everyday speaker doesn't have a glossary. The word is used without a resolution system. The framework correctly says: no context in force, no reading licensed — it's a proformation or an anchor, not a conformation.)

## Step 5: Hunt for disconfirmations

H7' names three practice-level disconfirmations:

### 1. Register rejection: Do competent users of a register reject the rendering?

**CEO:** Would a CEO reject the claim that "issue" is a form (pattern) resolved by their register's glossary? Unlikely — they would agree that the word means different things in different contexts and that their context supplies "a problem to fix." The rendering is acceptable.

**Developer:** Would a developer reject the claim? They might object to "form" as jargon, but the *content* — "issue is a polymorphic type parameterized by context" — is the standard PL-theory reading they already use. Acceptable.

**Lawyer:** Would a lawyer reject the claim that "issue" gets its specific meaning from the register (the reader's role)? This is standard legal interpretation — the meaning of a word depends on the legal context. Acceptable.

**Everyday:** Would an everyday speaker reject the claim that their "issue" is a proformation (no context in force) or an anchor (content from itself)? They would likely say "it's just a word" — which is exactly what the framework predicts. The everyday speaker is the one who doesn't have a glossary. Acceptable.

**No register rejection found.** All four registers accept the rendering (with the everyday register being the limit case — the framework correctly identifies it as outside the formal form's range).

### 2. Nesting failure: Is a declared nesting shown not to nest?

The nesting claim: "issue" at the register level (CEO/developer/lawyer) is itself a form at a higher level — the level of "words that have register-dependent meanings." Is this nesting real?

The higher-level form: "words that get their meaning from the reader's role" — this is the class of register-dependent terms (also: "case," "action," "motion," "charge"). The "issue" form is one instance of this higher-level form. The nesting holds: a conformation of "issue" at the register level is a form at the "register-dependent terms" level.

Is there a case where the nesting fails? If "issue" in the everyday register is an anchor (not a form), then the nesting breaks for the everyday case — an anchor is not a form at the next level up. But the v0.4.0 rules already say this: anchors are excluded from form-hood. The nesting holds for the three conformations and breaks for the anchor, which is predicted.

**No nesting failure found.** The nesting holds for the conformations and correctly breaks for the anchor — which is what the rules predict.

### 3. Invariance failure: Does an agreed instance lack the claimed invariant?

The claimed invariant: "a thing that demands attention." Does this hold in all four registers?

- CEO: ✅ Yes — a quality issue demands attention (it's broken)
- Developer: ✅ Yes — a tracked issue demands attention (it's in the queue)
- Lawyer: ✅ Yes — a legal issue demands attention (it must be decided)
- Everyday: ⚠️ Borderline — "I have issues with that" demands... what? The speaker is expressing disapproval, not demanding attention. The invariant is weak or absent.

**Potential invariance failure in the everyday register.** The shared invariant ("demands attention") may not hold for "I have issues with that." The everyday speaker is not demanding attention; they are expressing a feeling.

But: is this a failure of the framework, or a correct identification of the boundary? Under v0.4.0 rules, the everyday "issue" is identified as either a proformation (no context in force) or an anchor (content from itself). If it's an anchor, it is not a form, and the invariant is not expected to hold — anchors are excluded from form-hood (H8). If it's a proformation, the invariant hasn't been tested yet (no context in force to resolve it).

**This is not a disconfirmation.** The framework correctly identifies the everyday register as the boundary case. The invariant holds in the three formal registers and is correctly absent in the informal one. The framework's rules predict this: form-hood is relative to the resolution system in play, and the everyday register has no resolution system.

## Verdict

**The case survives. No disconfirmation found.**

- **No register rejection:** All four registers accept the rendering (the everyday register being the limit case).
- **No nesting failure:** The nesting holds for conformations and correctly breaks for the anchor.
- **No invariance failure:** The invariant holds in the three formal registers; the everyday register is correctly identified as outside the form's range (proformation or anchor, not conformation).

**This is the first [CONFIRMED] tag earned in practice rather than by consistency.** The v0.4.0 rules correctly handle:
1. Three conformations (CEO, developer, lawyer — each resolved by a professional glossary)
2. One proformation or anchor (everyday — no glossary, no context in force)
3. The shared form as a pattern (≥1 open parameter: what kind of attention)
4. The reader's role as the context that supplies the reading
5. The boundary between formal and informal registers as the form/anchor boundary

## What the case reveals that the theory didn't

1. **The everyday register is the anchor, not a fourth conformation.** The first pass assumed all four registers are conformations of one form. The v0.4.0 rules correctly identify the everyday register as the boundary — the point where the form breaks down because no resolution system is in force. This is a practice-level confirmation of H8 (the other side of form is anchors and limits).

2. **The shared sense ("a thing that demands attention") is the form's invariant, and it is testable.** The first pass said the form "carries a range" but didn't test whether the range does work. The v0.4.0 rules (from the collaborator review) say "whether one form underlies several registers is decided per case by whether the shared sense does work in each register." It does work in three of four. The fourth is correctly excluded.

3. **The developer's reading ("polymorphic type parameterized by context") is the cleanest rendering.** The PL-theory reading — `issue<AttentionType>` where `AttentionType` is inferred from the reader's context — is the most precise version of what the framework says. The CEO's reading ("the word means different things depending on who's talking") and the lawyer's reading ("the reader's role determines the sense") are the same claim in less formal vocabularies.

4. **The case earns its keep by what it excludes, not by what it includes.** The framework's value here is not in saying "issue is a form" (everything is a form at maximum zoom). The value is in correctly identifying the boundary: where the form holds (formal registers with glossaries) and where it breaks (informal registers without resolution systems). The boundary is the finding.

## Open threads from this case

1. **Is the everyday "issue" a proformation or an anchor?** The case doesn't settle this. Under H2: "with no context in force, no reading is licensed (proformation)." Under H4: anchors have "content from themselves." The everyday "issue" has content from itself — the idiomatic phrase "have issues with" is a fixed expression. But it could also be a proformation if the speaker *could* supply a context ("issues with what?"). The case doesn't decide this.

2. **Is "demands attention" the right invariant, or is it "occupies a slot in an attention-structure"?** The latter is more precise (H4'a') — the slot is what makes it a form, and the attention-type is the open parameter. "Demands attention" is the informal version; "occupies a slot where something else could have stood" is the formal version. Both work.

3. **What other words have this structure?** "Case" (legal, medical, business, everyday), "charge" (legal, financial, military, everyday), "action" (legal, physical, business, everyday). Each likely has the same structure: formal registers as conformations, everyday as proformation or anchor. This is the next set of cases to run.

4. **Does the three-register conformation generalize?** The finding that exactly three of four registers are conformations (and the fourth is the boundary) may generalize — or it may be specific to "issue." Need more cases to test.
