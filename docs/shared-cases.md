# Shared Cases — Run Through Every Deep-Tier Field

*Hypotheses referenced: H1 (form is the invariant), H2 (form resolved by context), H3 (context is what form is not), H4 (three conditions for form-ness), H5 (recursion), H6 (resolution is reversible), H7 (form is unprovable). See `starting-core.md` for full definitions.*

## Case 1: REGISTER — "issue" across registers

**The case:** The word "issue" as used by:
- A **CEO** ("we have a quality issue") — a problem, a malfunction
- A **developer** ("I created an issue for that") — a neutral unit of tracked work
- A **lawyer** ("the issue in this case is...") — a point in dispute, a legal question
- **Everyday speech** ("I have issues with that") — a personal objection or emotional difficulty

**Question:** Does register live in the form or in the context? Is "issue" the same form resolved by four contexts, or four different forms that share a word?

### Spencer-Brown

The mark "issue" is drawn once. Each register crosses the boundary into a different space — the CEO's space, the developer's, etc. The mark is the same; the space it indicates is different. Register lives in the *space* (context), not in the *mark* (form). [RIGOROUS] The form is "issue" as a unit of attention; the context (register) determines what kind of attention.

But Spencer-Brown would say the mark *creates* the space — the word "issue" calls the space into being. This is stronger than formology: the word doesn't just get resolved by the register; it *creates* the register by being spoken into it. [RESONANCE]

### Linguistics

Register is a well-studied concept (Halliday, Bühler). "Issue" is a single lexical item (type) with different senses in different registers. The senses are related (all involve "a thing that demands attention") but distinct. [RIGOROUS]

The formology question — "does register live in the form or in the context?" — has a clean linguistic answer: **both.** The form carries the *range* of possible senses (the sense potential); the context (register) *selects* which sense is active. This is **underspecification resolution** — the form is underspecified, and context fills in the specification. [RIGOROUS]

This is the strongest finding for the register case: formology's proformation ("complete but undetermined") maps to linguistic underspecification. The form is not "empty before context" — it carries a *range* of potential meanings. The context selects from that range. [CONFIRMED: the register case confirms that proformation is underspecification, not emptiness.]

### Frege

"Issue" has one sense (Frege would say: the mode of presentation — "a thing demanding attention") and multiple references (the specific thing in each register: the quality problem, the ticket, the legal point, the personal objection). [RIGOROUS]

But this is wrong for the lawyer case — "the issue in this case" has a *different sense* (a point of law in dispute) than the CEO's "quality issue" (a malfunction). The senses are not identical; they are related. Frege's framework handles this (sense is context-dependent in practice), but the clean form → sense / context → reference mapping breaks down when the form carries *different senses* in different contexts, not one sense with different references. [CONFIRMED: the register case reveals that form can carry multiple senses, not one sense — form is underspecified, not sense-uniform.]

### Peirce

"Issue" is a type; each use is a token. The tokens fall into different interpretants (CEO: "a problem to fix"; developer: "a unit of work"; lawyer: "a legal question"). [RIGOROUS]

Peirce's interpretant is what formology lacks — the *product* of resolution as distinct from the *state*. In the register case, the interpretant is the specific meaning produced in the hearer's mind. Formology's conformation is the form-in-context (the state of being resolved), not the meaning produced (the interpretant). [CONFIRMED: the register case reveals the missing interpretant — see stress test H4.]

### Aristotle

"Issue" is matter (the word as potential) that form (the register's determining principle) actualizes. The CEO's register gives "issue" the form of "problem"; the developer's gives it the form of "work unit." [RESONANCE]

But this inverts formology: in Aristotle, the register (the determining principle) is the form, and "issue" (the indeterminate substrate) is the matter. In formology, "issue" is the form and the register is the context. The same case, with roles swapped. [CONFIRMED: the register case confirms the Aristotelian inversion — formology's form = Aristotle's matter, formology's context = Aristotle's form.]

### Category theory

"Issue" is an object in a category. Each register is a functor mapping it to a different category (the category of problems, the category of work items, etc.). The form is the object; the context is the functor. [RESONANCE]

But the functors don't preserve structure — "issue" in the CEO's register has different relational properties than in the developer's register. If the functors don't preserve structure, they aren't functors. The mapping is to *functions* (arbitrary maps), not *functors* (structure-preserving maps). The category-theoretic formulation weakens under the register case. [AFFIRMED]

### Gentner (structure-mapping)

The four senses of "issue" share a relational structure at some depth: all involve "a thing that demands attention/response." The CEO and everyday senses share a shallow structure (attention + negative valence). The developer and lawyer senses share a deeper structure (attention + formality + procedure). [RIGOROUS]

The fork (where the senses stop sharing form) is at different depths for different pairs. The CEO/developer fork is shallow (both are "work to be done" — the valence differs). The developer/lawyer fork is deeper (both are "formal units in a process" — the process differs). [CONFIRMED: the register case confirms that the fork is at different depths for different pairs — metaphor magnitude is a profile, not a single number.]

### Lakoff and Johnson

"Issue" is a case of the metaphor PROBLEMS ARE OBJECTS ("I have an issue" — you can possess it, hold it, have it). The register differences are target-domain differences — the same source domain (objects that demand attention) mapped to different targets (quality, work tracking, legal disputes, personal psychology). [RESONANCE]

**Summary across fields:**

1. Register lives in **both** form and context — the form carries a *range* of potential senses (underspecification), and context selects from that range. [CONFIRMED by linguistics and Frege]
2. Proformation is **underspecification**, not emptiness. The form before context is not blank; it carries a range. [CONFIRMED by the register case]
3. Formology lacks the **interpretant** — the product of resolution as distinct from the state of being resolved. Peirce has this; formology doesn't. [CONFIRMED by Peirce]
4. The fork is at different depths for different pairs — metaphor magnitude is a **profile**, not a single number. [CONFIRMED by Gentner]
5. The Aristotelian **inversion** holds: formology's form = the underspecified substrate (Aristotle's matter); formology's context = the specifying principle (Aristotle's form). [CONFIRMED by Aristotle]

## Case 2: NESTING — note → chord → key → piece

**The case:** A musical note (B♭) in a chord (B♭ major) in a key (B♭ major) in a piece ("La Marseillaise"). Four levels of resolution, each nesting inside the next.

**Question:** Does resolution need to be indexed to a level? Can one resolved thing realize more than one form?

### Spencer-Brown

Each level is a distinction within a distinction. The note is a mark; the chord is a space in which the mark appears; the key is a larger space; the piece is the largest. Each distinction is nested. [RIGOROUS] The mark at each level is the same mark — but the space it indicates is different at each level. This is H5 (a resolved form is itself a form at the next level) — the note resolved by the chord is itself a form that the key resolves.

### Linguistics

This is **cataphora/anaphora chains** — "he" resolves to "John," "John" resolves to "the man who walked in," etc. Each resolution is a level. [RIGOROUS] The answer to "can one resolved thing realize more than one form" is **yes** — the note B♭ realizes the form "tonic" (at the chord level), "root" (at the key level), and "opening note" (at the piece level). It is the same note realizing different forms at different levels.

### Frege

At each level, the sense narrows and the reference stays the same. B♭ (the pitch) is the reference; "tonic," "root," "opening note" are increasingly specific senses. [RIGOROUS] But the senses are *different* — not refinements of one sense, but distinct modes of presentation. This means one reference can carry multiple senses depending on the level. [CONFIRMED: the nesting case confirms that resolution is level-indexed — the same thing can be resolved differently at different levels.]

### Peirce

Each level is a token of a different type: B♭ (type: pitch class), tonic (type: scale degree), opening note (type: musical role). [RIGOROUS] The same physical event (a note being played) is a token of multiple types simultaneously. This is the **multi-type realization** — one token, many types. [CONFIRMED: the nesting case confirms multi-type realization — one resolved thing can realize more than one form.]

### Aristotle

At each level, a different form actualizes the matter. The matter (the sound B♭) is actualized as tonic (by the chord's form), as root (by the key's form), as opening note (by the piece's form). [RESONANCE] The same matter receives multiple forms — but in Aristotle, the forms are hierarchical (the piece's form includes the key's form includes the chord's form). In formology, the levels are resolution levels, not inclusion levels. The nesting is the same structure but described differently.

### Gentner

The relational structure maps across levels: tonic-ness (the relation "is the first degree of the scale") is preserved from key to chord to piece (the tonic is still the tonic at every level). But the *specific* tonic changes (B♭ in B♭ major, C in C major). [RIGOROUS] The structure is the invariant; the specific values are the conformations. This is H1 at each level — form is what stays the same when the instance changes.

**Summary across fields:**

1. Resolution **is** level-indexed. The same thing resolves differently at each level. [CONFIRMED across all fields]
2. One resolved thing **can** realize more than one form — multi-type realization (Peirce) / multi-sense realization (Frege). [CONFIRMED]
3. H5 (recursion) is confirmed: each level's resolution is itself a form at the next level. [CONFIRMED]
4. The levels are not just nesting — they are **different forms** that the same thing realizes. This means H4's "substitution" condition needs to be level-aware: the note substitutes for "tonic" at the chord level, for "root" at the key level, for "opening note" at the piece level. [AFFIRMED: H4 needs a level parameter.]

## Case 3: NON-FORM — the strongest candidate that fails the form test

**The case:** Find something that fails H4's three conditions. The brief warns: if nothing fails, the definition is too broad.

### Candidates and testing:

**Candidate 1: A raw sensory quale (the redness of red).**
- (a) Structural slot? No — the quale doesn't occupy a position in a system; it is a phenomenal quality. **Fails (a).** [AFFIRMED]

**Candidate 2: A physical law (e.g., F = ma).**
- (a) Structural slot? Yes — it occupies a position in the relational structure of mechanics.
- (b) Substitutes for something recoverable? Yes — it stands in for the relationship between force, mass, and acceleration.
- (c) Gets meaning from context rather than from itself? **No** — F = ma carries its own meaning regardless of context. It is a literal, not a form. **Fails (c).** [CONFIRMED: a physical law is a literal, not a form. It carries its own meaning.]

But wait — H4's original formulation says: "If something fails the third condition (it carries its own meaning regardless of context), it is a literal — but literals are just forms in a context that doesn't demand resolution." So a physical law is a form in a context where the context doesn't demand resolution — the law is so well-established that the context is implicit. This is the escape clause, and it means **nothing fails the form test** because every literal is a form in a trivial context.

**Candidate 3: A proper name ("John").**
- (a) Structural slot? Yes — it occupies a referential position.
- (b) Substitutes for something recoverable? No — a proper name *directly refers*; it doesn't substitute for a recoverable antecedent. [RIGOROUS] (This is Kripke's rigid designation: a proper name refers to the same individual in every possible world, not by description but by baptismal chain.)
- (c) Gets meaning from context? No — a proper name gets its reference from its causal-historical chain, not from context. **Fails (b) and (c).** [CONFIRMED: a proper name is not a form. It is a rigid designator (Kripke). It does not substitute; it directly refers.]

**Candidate 4: A mathematical constant (π).**
- (a) Structural slot? Yes.
- (b) Substitutes? No — π *is* the ratio. It doesn't stand in for it.
- (c) Context-dependent meaning? No — π is π in every context. **Fails (b) and (c).** [CONFIRMED: mathematical constants are literals, not forms.]

**Verdict:** The strongest candidate that fails the form test is **a proper name (Kripke's rigid designator).** [CONFIRMED: proper names fail because they don't substitute — they directly refer. The form test successfully distinguishes forms from rigid designators.] The physical law and mathematical constant are saved by the escape clause (literals are forms in trivial contexts), but proper names are not — a proper name does not occupy a structural slot *that could be filled by something else*. It is *designated*, not *positioned*.

However, the escape clause makes the definition very broad. If literals are forms in trivial contexts, then "everything is form" is true — but only at maximum zoom, which the brief acknowledges is "true and useless there." The proper name is the one thing that resists even the escape clause, because a proper name is not "a form in a context that doesn't demand resolution" — it is a different kind of reference entirely (causal-historical, not descriptive).

## Case 4: METAPHOR TRIO — deep, shallow, disputed

**The case:** Pick one metaphor most people find deep, one shallow, one disputed. Test whether depth of fork ranks them the way intuition does.

**Deep metaphor: THE BRAIN IS A COMPUTER.**
- Most people find this deep — it structures how we think about cognition (memory, processing, inputs/outputs).
- Fork depth: the shared form (input → processing → output; storage → retrieval; representation) is deep — it holds through many specifics. The fork is at the level of implementation (silicon vs. neurons, serial vs. parallel, symbolic vs. connectionist).
- Intuition: deep. Fork: deep. **Match.**

**Shallow metaphor: MY BOSS IS A DICTATOR.**
- Most people find this shallow — it imports a few surface features (authoritarian, unilateral) without structural depth.
- Fork depth: the shared form (unilateral decision-making, lack of consultation) is shallow — it holds only at the surface. The fork is immediate (the boss doesn't have an army, doesn't control the press, etc.).
- Intuition: shallow. Fork: shallow. **Match.**

**Disputed metaphor: A COMPANY IS A LIVING ORGANISM.**
- Some find this deep (homeostasis, growth, adaptation, death, reproduction as spin-offs); others find it shallow (metaphorical comfort without predictive power).
- Fork depth: the shared form (boundary maintenance, energy processing, internal coordination, response to environment) is **middle-depth.** It holds through several levels of specifics (organizational homeostasis maps to biological homeostasis; org adaptation maps to evolutionary adaptation) but forks at the level of mechanism (organizations don't reproduce sexually, don't have DNA, don't metabolize).
- Intuition: disputed. Fork: middle. **Match** — the disputed metaphor is middle-depth, which is why people disagree (it's neither obviously deep nor obviously shallow).

**Summary across fields:**

1. The metaphor trio is ranked consistently by intuition and fork depth: deep → deep, shallow → shallow, disputed → middle. [CONFIRMED]
2. The fork depth is a **single scale for this case** — all three metaphors can be ranked on one axis. [AFFIRMED]
3. But the register case (Case 1) showed the fork is at different depths for different *pairs* — which means the fork is a **profile across pairs**, not a single number. The two findings are compatible: for a single pair, the fork is a number; across pairs, it's a profile. [CONFIRMED: metaphor magnitude is a profile, not a number — but a single pair has a single depth.]

Gentner's structure-mapping confirms: the systematicity of a metaphor is its depth of shared relational structure, which is what the fork measures. [RIGOROUS]

## Case 5: BUSINESS — a purchase order through a company

**The case:** A generic purchase order (PO) moves through a mid-sized company: request → approval → issue → fulfill → receive → invoice → pay. What does formology reveal that standard process mapping doesn't?

### What standard process mapping sees:

A linear process with steps, gates, actors, and documents. Each step transforms the PO's state (draft → submitted → approved → issued → fulfilled → received → invoiced → paid). The PO is a document that moves through a workflow.

### What formology sees:

The PO is a **form** — it is what stays the same through the process (the same PO number, the same line items, the same supplier). At each step, a different **context** is declared: the requester's context (need), the approver's context (budget authority), the supplier's context (fulfillment), the accounts payable context (payment). Each context **resolves** the PO into a different **conformation**:

- In the requester's context: the PO is a *need* (proformation → conformation: "I need these items")
- In the approver's context: the PO is a *budget commitment* (proformation → conformation: "this commits $X from budget Y")
- In the supplier's context: the PO is an *order* (proformation → conformation: "ship these items to this address")
- In the AP context: the PO is a *liability* (proformation → conformation: "we owe $X to supplier Z")

### What formology reveals that process mapping doesn't:

1. **The PO is the same form at every step.** Process mapping sees different documents (requisition, PO, invoice, payment). Formology sees one form (the PO) in different conformations (need, commitment, order, liability). The form persists; the conformations change. This is H1 — and it reveals that process mapping *fragments* the form by treating each step as a different document rather than as the same form resolved by a different context.

2. **The POSIWID gap is visible.** The *designed purpose* of the PO process (proformation — what the process was designed to do: control spending, ensure approval, track fulfillment) vs. the *actual behavior* (conformation — what the process does in practice: rubber-stamp approvals, rush orders that bypass the process, invoices that don't match POs). The gap between design and actual is where the process is alive — where S4 (intelligence) should be sensing the difference and feeding it back.

3. **Each conformation carries a different register.** The same PO is spoken about differently in each context — "the need," "the commitment," "the order," "the liability." These are register differences (Case 1) applied to the same form. Formology reveals that the process is not just a state machine — it is a form traversing registers, and the register determines the vocabulary, the stakeholders, and the authority structure.

4. **The process is recursive (H5).** Each step in the PO process is itself a form with its own conformations. The approval step is a form (the approval workflow) that resolves into conformations (approved, rejected, returned for revision). The PO process contains sub-processes, each of which is a form. Standard process mapping sees this as nested flowcharts; formology sees it as the same recursion at every level — H5.

5. **Formology reveals the context as the active agent.** In process mapping, the PO moves through steps. In formology, the contexts (the departments, the roles, the systems) are the active agents that resolve the form. The PO doesn't "move" — it gets resolved by successive contexts. This inverts the process-mapping intuition: the form is passive; the context is active. (This is the Aristotelian inversion — formology's form is the matter; formology's context is the form.)

### What formology does NOT reveal that process mapping does:

- Timing and sequencing (how long each step takes, what happens in parallel)
- Actor assignment (who does what)
- Exception handling (what happens when the process breaks)
- Metrics and SLAs (how to measure performance)

Formology is complementary to process mapping, not a replacement. Process mapping gives you the mechanics; formology gives you the *what persists and what changes* — the form and the conformations. [AFFIRMED: formology adds the form/conformation framing, the POSIWID gap, the register analysis, and the recursion; it does not replace process mechanics.]

**Summary across fields:**

The business case confirms formology's value proposition: it reveals what standard process mapping doesn't — the persistence of form across conformations, the POSIWID gap as a viability condition, the register structure of a process, and the recursion. But it also reveals the Aristotelian inversion: in practice, the *context* (the departments, the roles) is the active agent, and the *form* (the PO) is the passive substrate. This means H2 ("form gets its meaning from context") is more accurate than H1 ("form is what stays the same") for describing the business case — because the form doesn't *do* anything; it *gets resolved*. [CONFIRMED: the business case confirms the tension between H1 (form as active invariant) and H2–H3 (form as passive, resolved substrate).]
